How this trainer is built

Every mechanic in this trainer traces to a specific, well-replicated finding about how memory actually works. This page names the finding, then names the feature that implements it — so if something here feels annoying or inefficient, you can see why it's deliberate.

HIGH utility · practice testing HIGH utility · distributed practice LOW utility · rereading LOW utility · highlighting

Of the ten study techniques Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed for Psychological Science in the Public Interest (2013), only two earned a "high utility" rating: practice testing and distributed practice. Rereading, highlighting and summarising — what most students actually do — came out low. This whole site is built out of the two that won.

1. Testing is not measurement. It's the studying.

Roediger and Karpicke's work found that repeated studying produced no benefit for delayed recall, while repeated testing produced a large one — even though the re-readers had far more exposure to the material. Students consistently don't believe this, which is why most people reread instead of self-quizzing.

✅what that means here The trainer is 180 questions and no reading passages. Even the cram sheet hides its answers behind a tap, so a "review" is still an act of retrieval rather than a re-read.

2. Getting it right once is not learning it.

Rawson and Dunlosky's successive relearning studies combine retrieval practice with spacing: you retrieve an item correctly to criterion, and then you do it again in later, separate sessions. In a real introductory course, concepts trained that way beat the ones students studied on their own by more than a full letter grade on the actual course exam. It has since been replicated in a difficult upper-level course and in K–12 classrooms.

✅what that means here A question does not retire until you have answered it correctly on three different days. The counter is on every explanation card ("retrieved correctly 2/3 sessions"), and Spaced review only serves items that have not hit criterion yet. Getting something right today advances it one step — it does not remove it.

3. Gaps beat cramming — and the right gap depends on your deadline.

Cepeda and colleagues synthesised 839 assessments of distributed practice across 317 experiments, then mapped the optimum directly: the best gap between study sessions is roughly 10–20% of how long you need to remember the material (about 20% at a delay of a few weeks, falling toward 5% at a year).

✅what that means here Review gaps expand 1 → 3 → 7 → 16 days. Enter your exam date on the review page and every gap is recomputed as ~20% of the days you have left, then clamped so no review is ever scheduled after the exam. Two weeks out, that's a ~3-day gap; three days out, it's daily.

4. Mixing topics feels worse and works better.

In Taylor and Rohrer's experiments, interleaving practice hurt performance during the practice session and then doubled scores on a test the next day. The mechanism matters for A&P: interleaving specifically improves your ability to work out which concept a question is about — exactly the skill a mixed multiple-choice exam tests, where the hard part is telling preload from afterload, or stenosis from incompetence, when nobody tells you which chapter you're in.

✅what that means here Both forms, the starred set and the review queue are shuffled across all five topics every time. The Topic drill is the one blocked-practice tool on the site — useful for repairing a weak area, but it should never be the last thing you do before the exam. Finish on a mixed form.

5. The answers you were sure about — and wrong about — are gold.

Butterfield and Metcalfe's hypercorrection effect: errors committed with high confidence are corrected better than low-confidence errors, because the mismatch between what you expected and what you got makes you attend hard to the feedback. The catch from the follow-up work is that high-confidence errors also tend to return after a week — so one correction isn't enough, they need re-testing.

✅what that means here You rate confidence before the answer is revealed. A miss you marked "sure" gets flagged on the spot, counted in the calibration panel at the end of the run, and pushed to the front of the review queue — because it will come back if you leave it. That is also why the trainer asks you to say the correction out loud instead of just reading it.

6. Feedback, immediately and specifically.

The hypercorrection benefit depends on getting prompt, specific correction — not just "wrong."

✅what that means here Every question reveals the correct choice, restates it in full, explains why it's right, and names the learning objective it came from — on the same screen, one tap after you answer.

7. Produce the explanation; don't just recognise it.

Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation — asking yourself why a fact is true — earned moderate support in the same review, and they cost nothing to add to a quiz.

✅what that means here After a miss the trainer holds you for one beat and asks you to say why the right answer is right in your own words before moving on. The cram sheet is built cue-first for the same reason: you generate the answer, then check it.

What this trainer deliberately does NOT do

  • No highlighting, no summarising, no rereading long text. All three rated low utility. There is almost no prose here to reread on purpose.
  • Only one mnemonic. Keyword mnemonics rated low utility for durable learning and are narrow in scope. The one mnemonic on the cram sheet ("Never let monkeys eat bananas") is there because the instructor put it on a slide, which makes it a testable item rather than a study strategy.
  • No streaks, no XP, no daily flame. Nothing here rewards you for showing up twice in one day when the evidence says to come back tomorrow instead.

So what should you actually do?

  • Tonight: sit Form A cold, confidence rating on. Clear every miss.
  • Then: check Objective Scope, and drill only the topics that came back weak.
  • Every day after: open Spaced review and clear what's due. It'll be short. That's the point — and it's the single highest-value habit on this list.
  • 2–3 days out: sit Form B cold as a dress rehearsal.
  • Night before: the starred set, then cues-only on the cram sheet. Sleep.
